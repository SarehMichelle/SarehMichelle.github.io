What brand video production costs for a tour operator

By Sareh Michelle. November 1, 2026.

The four things that move the price of a brand video shoot for a tour or activity business, and how to brief one so the money goes into footage, not waiting around.

Brand video production for a tour operator is priced from a brief, not a rate card, because a half-day on a catamaran and a multi-day story across two islands are not the same job. What moves the number is time on location, the size of the crew, the cast, and what has to be delivered at the end. Understand those four and you can brief a shoot that spends the budget on footage instead of on waiting around.

1. Time on location

The biggest line in any production is the day itself. A half-day shoot joins one of your tours that is already running and captures what happens. A single-day shoot is planned end to end, with a schedule built around the light. A multi-day production covers several locations or a larger story, with travel between them.

Time is also where operators waste the most money without meaning to. A crew that arrives at 7 a.m. for a 10 a.m. departure, or films a tour on a day the swell is up and the boat cannot get to the coast, has spent the budget and brought back half the shots. This is why the brief fixes dates against your season and the weather windows for your coast, and why a good production company talks to your captain before it talks to you about the shot list.

2. Crew

A small, experienced crew beats a large one on a tour. Two or three people can cover a boat deck, the water and the air without getting in the guests’ way. Six cannot. Crew size goes up when the brief calls for it: a dedicated drone operator, a second camera for a two-location day, a stills photographer so the same shoot covers photography as well as film.

Ask who is on the crew and what each person is there to do. If you get a confident answer, the crew is sized for the brief. If you get a shrug, the price is probably sized for the videographer’s calendar.

3. Cast

Real guests make the best footage and they cost nothing, but they came for the tour, not for you. Guests are never asked to do anything beyond what they came for, and anyone who appears in the work signs a release. For a planned shoot, a small cast of two to four people who have agreed to be filmed gives the crew the freedom to ask for the bow, the first jump or the walk down the trail again in better light. Casting is a line in the brief, and it is one of the cheaper ways to raise the quality of the finished film.

4. Deliverables

The same day on location can produce very different sets of files, and the edit is where the second half of the budget goes. Be specific. For most tour operators the useful list is:

  • A project film, 60 to 120 seconds, for the website and paid campaigns.
  • A set of reels, cut vertically for each platform, each built around one moment.
  • A photo set for listings, the booking page and ads.
  • Raw footage, if you want it, which should be agreed up front along with who owns what.

Ask for the deliverables in writing, with formats, before you agree a price. Ownership and usage should be written into the agreement at the same time.

What you should expect to receive

A finished brand video production should give you content that works for at least a year. The project film goes on the website, the booking page and into paid campaigns. The reels run on social for months, one at a time, each one doing a different job in the path from the first scroll to the booking. The photos replace the phone shots on your listings. If a quote only includes one long film, you are paying for a day of production and getting one asset out of it.
